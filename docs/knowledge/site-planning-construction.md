## What site planning involves (Q141)

Site planning arranges buildings, roads, parking, utilities, drainage, and open space within the property’s limits. Good site planning requires practical design skill. Efficient street layouts, including placing development on both sides of streets where appropriate, can improve land use. On sloping property, grading, retaining walls, and usable pad areas become especially important. The design team needs to understand how these elements work together.

## Whether a layout is practical to build (Q142)

Review grading, equipment access, retaining walls, drainage facilities, utility routes, and construction space. Experience helps identify missing components and areas that may be difficult to build. Utility coordination is especially important on dense multifamily sites. Sewer, water, storm drains, irrigation, fire lines, and dry utilities may all compete for limited space. A three-dimensional review helps identify crossings, depth conflicts, and required separation before construction begins.

## Building placement and grading (Q143)

Start with a grading design that considers roads, drainage, slopes, walls, and building pads. A key goal is to balance cut and fill where practical and reduce unnecessary soil import or export. On sloping sites, retaining walls and slopes reduce the area available for buildings. Required setbacks may be measured from property lines, slopes, or pad edges, depending on the applicable requirements. Those limits need to be understood before building locations are finalized.

## Roads and driveways (Q144)

Coordinate roads and driveways with building entrances, garages, utilities, drainage, and emergency access. On steep sites, street and driveway grades need close attention. The allowable grades must be confirmed with the agencies, and the layout also needs to function comfortably for residents. Steep driveways can make parking, opening car doors, and walking more difficult. Practical use matters alongside meeting design requirements.

## Parking, circulation, and access (Q145)

Review parking together with turning movements, pedestrian routes, accessible access, deliveries, and emergency vehicle movement. A grid layout can provide straightforward circulation. Cul-de-sacs require enough turning space, based on the applicable agency standards and vehicle requirements. On multifamily projects, parking can sometimes be arranged along open space edges to use the site efficiently, while maintaining access, landscaping, and other required clearances.

## Usable land and constrained areas (Q146)

Use a constraints map showing environmental areas, easements, setbacks, slopes, drainage features, and other limits. After those areas are identified, evaluate the remaining land for streets, lots, and buildings. Some constrained areas may serve as open space where allowed. The remaining usable land may have an awkward shape. That is where a skilled planner and civil engineer can make a significant difference.

## Retaining walls (Q147)

Retaining walls can make development possible on tight or sloping sites, but they add structural, foundation, drainage, and construction costs. Where space allows, slopes or a combination of slopes and shorter walls may be more economical. Dense multifamily projects often have less flexibility. Building a new retaining wall beside an existing wall creates additional challenges. The design may need to account for the existing structure and provide temporary shoring or support during construction.

## Drainage and building placement (Q148)

Establish the grading, streets, storm drains, detention, and water quality systems before finalizing building placement. These improvements help define the net usable pad area. Buildings can then be plotted within that area after the other constraints are considered. At the same time, begin with the intended building product in mind. The infrastructure and grading design must support the space each building needs.

## Utility routes (Q149)

New utility systems need to be coordinated for space, depth, separation, and building connections. Connecting new facilities around existing utilities is often more challenging. Use potholing where needed to verify the location and depth of existing facilities. That information can then be incorporated into the plans so new utilities are designed around actual field conditions.

## Shared spaces and amenities by phase (Q150)

Identify when each amenity will be completed, how it will be accessed, and who will be responsible for it. On single-family projects, parks and shared facilities may be delivered in phases and transferred or annexed into the homeowners association. Opening them must be coordinated with the applicable approvals and ownership arrangements. Apartment and multifamily rental projects may follow a different process. Amenities can open when the necessary work, approvals, and safe access are complete, consistent with the project’s occupancy requirements.

## Constructability review (Q151)

Constructability review checks whether the design can be built efficiently with the available space, access, materials, equipment, and sequence of work. For example, underground detention systems vary in cost, delivery time, and installation requirements. Comparing suppliers early can affect both budget and schedule. Other reviews may address temporary shoring beside existing walls, excavation access, or the placement of water meters and hydrants in tight spaces. The review should also consider how the completed improvements will operate.

## Problems a constructability review can uncover (Q152)

It can reveal utility conflicts, missing elevations, inadequate access, difficult installation sequences, and missing construction details. Place particular emphasis on three-dimensional coordination of sewer, water, and storm drain systems. Plans should clearly identify manhole elevations, pipe invert elevations, and inlet and outlet elevations at drainage structures. Contractors need enough detail to build the system without guessing.

## When construction input should enter design (Q153)

Construction input should begin early and continue at major design milestones. It is useful to have contractors who specialize in the relevant work review the plans during development. Sewer, water, storm drain, and street contractors may identify different practical issues. Early input is particularly valuable for complex facilities such as lift stations, pump stations, and reservoirs, where access and construction sequencing can strongly affect the design.

## Keeping design within budget (Q154)

Establish a budget and update it as the design develops. Each consultant should understand the budget for their scope and provide estimates that can be compared with it. Agency standards may limit material choices, but approved alternatives can sometimes reduce costs. For example, PVC pipe may be an option in place of ductile iron where the agency and design requirements allow it. Wall finishes, landscaping, and shade structures also deserve close review. Value engineering should focus on meeting project needs while controlling cost.

## Comparing alternative layouts (Q155)

Compare the layouts using consistent assumptions for unit count, building type, roads, grading, utilities, and cost. A two-dimensional plan often reveals major differences in efficiency. Three-dimensional analysis is especially useful when terrain, wall heights, or utility conflicts could change the result. The preferred layout should be evaluated for both how much development it supports and how practical it is to build.

## Long-term maintenance (Q156)

First identify who will maintain each improvement. Public agencies typically establish standards for facilities they will accept and maintain. Private improvements need their own review of access, reliability, operating cost, and replacement needs. On one project, a proposed folding entry gate would have required large motors and opened too slowly. Brian helped change the design to conventional sliding gates that opened to each side of the driveway. That change reduced cost and improved operation and expected maintenance.

## Tracking design revisions (Q157)

Distinguish between revisions made during plan review and changes made after approval. Both need clear dates, revision labels, and records of the decisions behind them. Current CAD files are especially important for grading contractors and surveyors. Authorized changes need to be incorporated promptly and distributed to affected parties. Post-approval changes must also follow the required review process before they are released for construction.

## What a site plan should tell a nontechnical owner (Q158)

The owner should understand where buildings will go, how people will enter and move through the property, and where utilities, drainage, and constrained areas are located. Walk the owner through the plan in everyday language. Explain how the parts connect, where space is tight, and which areas need extra attention. The goal is to help the owner understand how the project will be built and how it will function when complete.

## Layout changes that made a development more practical (Q159)

Adjusting grading and wall heights can reduce cost where the site has enough room. In some situations, additional grading is more economical than taller retaining walls. Utility routing can also be improved. Shorter sewer or storm drain routes, or more direct inlet connections, may reduce pipe length and simplify construction. Each change needs to be checked against drainage, grading, approvals, and the rest of the site design.

## Early coordination preventing later rework (Q160)

Review plans closely and cross-check elevations between related drawings. A tracking matrix can help compare sewer lateral elevations and other connection points. On a recent review, Brian questioned the bottom elevation of a pump manhole. The drawing appeared to require a structure about 15.5 feet tall. After checking with the engineer, the team confirmed that the elevation was incorrect and the intended height was about 10.5 feet. That example shows the value of an independent quality control review.

## Horizontal land development (Q161)

Horizontal development includes clearing, demolition, grading, building pads, retaining walls, utilities, drainage, roads, sidewalks, and related site improvements. On larger projects, it can also include bridges, sewer lift stations, pump stations, and other major infrastructure. Dry utilities, overhead utility relocation, and undergrounding can also be part of the work. Contracts need to clearly identify where each contractor’s work begins and ends, especially at building connections and where trades share responsibilities.

## Sequence of site preparation (Q162)

Plan the sequence around grading, utility depths, access, and the space available for crews to work. Work generally begins with clearing and rough grading. Deep drainage facilities and buried basins may need to go in before other utilities. Sewer, remaining storm drain work, water, dry utilities, and streets follow in a coordinated sequence. Perimeter walls, retaining walls, and off-site improvements can often move forward at the same time. The goal is to keep work moving without having one contractor block or damage another contractor’s work.

## Before grading begins (Q163)

Confirm approved plans, permits, required conditions, fees, bonds, environmental protections, and inspection arrangements. At the pre-construction meeting, verify the current plan revision, including its delta number, and confirm that every contractor has the same set. The grading contractor also needs the current rough grading CAD files to develop a digital terrain model for machine-controlled equipment. This can reduce staking needs, but survey coordination remains important. Brian generally plans for at least 48 hours’ notice to the surveyor, subject to the project’s arrangements.

## Demolition and clearing (Q164)

Identify what will be removed, what will remain, and which utilities need to be disconnected or protected. Existing utilities that remain must be clearly marked. Heavy equipment crossings should be limited to designated locations with protection appropriate for the equipment loads. On projects Brian has managed, crossings have been limited to two controlled locations. Before demolition, applicable hazardous-material work, well abandonment, and septic pumping and removal must be addressed. Required permits and approvals need to be coordinated with the responsible agencies.

## Coordinating grading with civil and geotechnical professionals (Q165)

Coordination begins during bidding. Contractors receive the grading plans, geotechnical recommendations, and CAD files so they can prepare their own takeoffs and pricing. After the contract is awarded, the civil engineer, surveyor, geotechnical engineer, and contractor review expectations at the pre-construction meeting. The geotechnical engineer needs to accept the exposed removal areas before fill placement begins. Surveying those areas also helps establish actual removal quantities. In Brian’s typical contract approach, remedial excavation is measured, while mass grading and finishing are fixed-price scopes. Import and export remain potential variables, depending on the contract and how the soil actually performs.

## Rough grading vs finished grading (Q166)

Rough grading establishes the main shape and elevations of the site. It also includes required removal of unsuitable material, remedial excavation, and recompaction under the geotechnical recommendations. Finished grading brings the ground to the elevations needed for drainage, concrete flatwork, and landscaping. Around buildings, fine grading may require several return visits. Some work happens after scaffolding is removed, often around the stucco stage, so final surfaces can be completed without interference.

## Soil import or export (Q167)

A site is often designed to balance its cut and fill using an assumed soil shrinkage factor. If the soil shrinks differently than expected, the project may need imported soil or have excess soil to export. That adds costs for loading, hauling, soil purchase or disposal, testing, and access. During bidding, obtain import and export unit prices with clear quantity assumptions. Also identify possible soil sources and receiving sites. Testing must address suitability for the intended use and potential environmental concerns.

## Earthwork assumptions that need closer review (Q168)

Compare the civil engineer’s earthwork calculations with the grading contractor’s independent takeoff. The engineer’s calculations include assumptions about remedial grading and soil shrinkage. The contractor reviews those assumptions, develops quantities, and identifies practical construction issues. In Brian’s experience, a detailed contractor takeoff can help refine the site balance. The design team may then adjust pad elevations, where feasible and approved, to reduce import or export. Large hauling operations also need early review of routes, access, and applicable permits.

## When actual conditions differ from assumptions (Q169)

Unexpected conditions can change grading quantities, costs, design, and the schedule. Manage that risk by reviewing actual conditions as grading progresses and updating the earthwork balance. This helps the team identify potential import or export needs before they become major surprises. When conditions differ, the team must document them, follow contract notice procedures, and obtain the necessary professional direction before changing the work.

## Excavation near existing underground utilities (Q170)

Coordination starts with required utility notifications, available records, field locating, and appropriate methods to verify utility locations. The contractor needs to coordinate protection, crossings, and any shutdowns with the utility owners. Plans and surface markings alone do not establish every utility’s exact position or depth. Those uncertainties need to be resolved before excavation reaches the affected area.

## Organizing utility installation (Q171)

Organize installation around utility depths, crossings, available work areas, and the overall grading plan. Deep drainage structures may go in early, followed by sewer, remaining storm drain facilities, water, dry utilities, and streets. Walls can often be built alongside that work. Smaller sites may not have enough space for multiple crews to work efficiently. On some smaller projects Brian has managed, each major utility component took roughly three to four weeks. Larger projects can support two or three crews in separate areas, which creates more opportunities to overlap the work.

## Water service (Q172)

Contact the water provider early to review existing facilities, available capacity, and potential upgrades. Those findings become more detailed as the project moves through design. Fire flow can drive water-system sizing more than household demand. Nearby storage, pressure, and distribution capacity all matter. On some projects, Brian has worked with a fire-flow requirement of 1,500 gallons per minute at 20 psi residual pressure. That is a project example, not a standard to assume for every development. Responsibility for major facilities also needs to be confirmed with the provider early.

## Sewer service (Q173)

Sewer planning depends on the connection location, available capacity, pipe elevations, and the slope needed for gravity flow. On many projects Brian has managed, the direction of flow and connection point were straightforward. Longer extensions or difficult elevations can create more complicated problems. Confirm the discharge route and elevations early so the grading and building plans support the sewer design.

## Electrical service (Q174)

Electrical planning starts with an application and accurate load information. That includes buildings, panel sizes, motorized gates, EV charging, and other equipment. The provider uses that information to determine service needs, transformer locations, and whether sufficient capacity is available. On Edison projects, Brian has worked through an overall capacity review described as a global analysis, followed by the applicable distribution design process, including Rule 15 work. He uses a dry utility consultant to help coordinate preliminary layouts, applications, utility design, and the development schedule.

## Storm drain installation (Q175)

Question reconstructed from the surrounding transcript. Storm drain work needs to be coordinated with grading, sewer crossings, buried structures, and the timing of street construction. If grading is complete but permanent drainage facilities are not operational, temporary measures must manage runoff during construction. As permanent pipes, structures, and streets are completed, the drainage system can take on its intended function. Until then, the project needs temporary controls suited to the active construction stage.

## Road construction and utility work (Q176)

The sequence depends partly on where the dry utilities will be installed. On residential subdivisions with enough parkway space, dry utilities can often run behind the curb. Street crossings can be installed first, allowing paving to proceed before the remaining behind-curb utility work. On tighter multifamily or apartment sites, main dry utility runs may be located in the street. Those installations need to be completed before paving, which changes the schedule.

## Access for equipment and deliveries (Q177)

Coordinate overall access and staging so contractors have room to receive materials, unload, and work. Each contractor plans the delivery and handling needs for its operation. Wet utility contractors need access for pipe, structures, and stockpiles. Dry utility contractors often establish a storage area for conduit and equipment. Those plans must fit together as construction changes the available routes and work areas.

## Contractors in different parts of the site (Q178)

Organize the work as a moving production line. For example, sewer work may begin in one area. Once that crew has completed enough of the site, the storm drain crew can start behind it. Water work can then follow as space becomes available. This lets trades progress through the project without waiting for every system to be finished across the entire site. The spacing between crews must still allow safe access, inspections, and efficient work.

## Whether a phase is ready for the next stage (Q179)

Check the work against the approved plans, required inspections, testing, and the needs of the next contractor. On accelerated schedules, different trades may work in separate portions of the site at the same time. That requires close coordination with inspectors and clear decisions about which areas can be released. Some system testing occurs after a larger section is complete. Required inspections and hold points still need to be respected before work is covered or the next activity proceeds.

## Horizontal construction management example (Q180)

One example is a large master-planned community Brian worked on in South Corona. The first phase included 540 lots and was completed in approximately nine months. Rough grading took about two to two and a half months, with production sometimes reaching approximately 100,000 cubic yards per day. The work required a master schedule that coordinated backbone roads and infrastructure with improvements inside the individual tracts. The key was overlapping work in separate areas while maintaining access, quality, and coordination between contractors. The total community lot count from the transcript is unverified and must not be published until Brian confirms it.

## Off-site improvements (Q181)

Off-site improvements can include road widening, frontage improvements, sidewalks, utility extensions, drainage connections, traffic signals, and connections to existing public systems. Larger master-planned communities may also require bridges, reservoirs, sewer lift stations, pump stations, or upgrades to existing facilities. Identify potential obligations during due diligence and refine them through the approval process. The scope must be included in the project’s budget and schedule.

## Frontage improvements (Q182)

Frontage work can add curbs, sidewalks, paving, landscaping, drainage, and utility relocation. An agency may also require pavement cores to evaluate the existing street section. If the existing pavement is inadequate, the project may need removal and replacement in addition to widening. Traffic control is a major part of planning both street improvements and utility connections in existing roads.

## Road widening (Q183)

Road widening requirements need to be identified during the entitlement and tentative map process. The work may require additional right-of-way dedication, pavement, drainage changes, and relocation or undergrounding of overhead utilities. Account for those requirements early so the final map and improvement plans reflect them. That reduces the chance of late changes affecting the interior development layout.

## Utility extensions (Q184)

Utility extensions involve design, approvals, access rights, materials, installation, inspection, and acceptance. Existing utility conflicts can make construction more difficult. New lines may need to pass above, below, or between existing facilities. Traffic control can also add substantial cost and time. Some work requires multiple stages, with traffic moved from one side of the road to the other as construction progresses.

## Work beyond the property boundaries (Q185)

Confirm the necessary property rights, permits, utility coordination, traffic control, and restoration requirements before work begins. Off-site work must be coordinated with the agencies, utility owners, and affected property owners. The budget also needs to include street restoration. Depending on the approved requirements, that may involve trench repairs, grinding and overlay, or more extensive pavement replacement.

## Why utility coordination is difficult (Q186)

Utility providers differ in how they handle design, contracting, construction, and scheduling. On a Fontana project Brian managed, the water provider prepared the plans and contracted for the work, while the developer paid the costs. That gave the team less direct control over the schedule. That differed from other projects where the team arranged the design, obtained provider approval, and hired the installation contractor. Understanding the provider’s process early is essential.

## Utility work performed by outside agencies (Q187)

Obtain the provider’s schedule, contractor contact information, and prerequisites as early as possible. Include those milestones in the project schedule and track the submissions, payments, and site conditions needed to keep the work moving. When the provider’s schedule does not match the project schedule, adjust related activities and stay in close contact with its team. A developer cannot assume that an outside agency’s crews will follow the developer’s preferred schedule.

## Temporary utilities (Q188)

Identify the temporary water, power, sanitation, and other services needed for each phase, then plan the transition to permanent service. On a Fontana project, the team used a temporary water arrangement with multiple manifolded meters to provide fire flow while the permanent off-site connection was being completed. That arrangement supported construction of the buildings while the permanent system progressed. Similar arrangements require project-specific review and approval from the responsible providers and authorities.

## Drainage changes affecting another part of the site (Q189)

Grading and drainage changes can redirect water toward unfinished pads, roads, or neighboring properties. Plan temporary runoff control for the whole site, including areas where permanent drainage is not yet operational. Temporary desilting basins and other best management practices, or BMPs, help manage runoff as construction moves from grading through utilities and paving.

## Erosion control and the construction sequence (Q190)

Erosion controls need to change as the site changes. Keep the field plan current so the team knows where permanent drainage is operating, where temporary measures remain necessary, and where BMPs need to be added or maintained. During active work, especially in rainy periods, that can require daily review. The controls need to function in the field and remain consistent with the project’s stormwater requirements.

## Seasonal conditions (Q191)

For wet weather, focus on functioning BMPs, temporary basins, drainage routes, and preparation before storms arrive. Major storms can exceed the capacity of temporary controls. The team needs a response plan based on site conditions and applicable requirements, rather than assuming that one rainfall threshold applies everywhere. During dry weather, dust control becomes a major concern. Coordinate contractor responsibilities for water trucks and watering so exposed areas receive adequate attention.

## Constrained access and equipment (Q192)

Limited access can affect equipment size, delivery routes, staging, and the order of construction. On a Chatsworth project, the team was building two bridges across a creek. An existing bridge could not support the heavy equipment needed for the work. They addressed that limitation by constructing an additional crossing structure over the existing bridge, allowing equipment access while the permanent bridges were built. That example shows why access problems need an engineered construction solution early in the schedule.

## Site inspections and required testing (Q193)

Connect inspection and testing needs to the construction schedule and give the responsible professionals advance notice. The geotechnical team is involved during grading, utility trench backfill, and preparation for streets and sidewalks. The construction manager keeps that team informed about upcoming work so required observations and tests happen at the right time and do not delay the next operation.

## Who determines whether site work meets requirements (Q194)

Acceptance involves the responsible design professionals, geotechnical team, agency inspectors, and utility providers within their respective roles. Grading requires the applicable verification of elevations and geotechnical acceptance. Water systems undergo required pressure testing, disinfection, and water-quality testing before service. Sewer systems receive the tests and inspections required by the accepting agency, which may include air and mandrel testing. Storm drains may require video inspection and other acceptance checks. Coordinate those steps and make sure the required reports reach the appropriate parties.

## Tracking inspection corrections (Q195)

Track each correction by location, responsible contractor, required action, and completion status. The geotechnical team documents unacceptable soil conditions or failed compaction tests. Utility and street inspectors identify corrections within their areas of responsibility. The contractor completes the correction, and the appropriate reviewer verifies it. The item remains open until the required acceptance is obtained.

## Documenting underground work before it is covered (Q196)

Use dated photographs, measurements, inspection records, test results, and updated drawings tied to clear locations. Drone images can also help show progress and timing. Required inspections need to occur before backfill. Depending on the system, those may include checks of pipe installation, thrust blocks, structures, and testing. If an emergency creates a need to cover work before the inspector is available, coordinate with the responsible authority and document the conditions thoroughly. Photographs alone do not replace required acceptance.

## Final paving and building construction (Q197)

Distinguish between the initial paving needed for construction access and the final surface course. On projects Brian has managed, fire access and an operational water supply were required before building construction could proceed. The team installed the initial asphalt course to support construction and emergency access. The final asphalt surface was placed as buildings became substantially complete. That required moving equipment, restricting parking, and coordinating paving in phases. Pavement thickness and access requirements must follow the approved project plans.

## Protecting completed improvements (Q198)

Establish protected routes, storage areas, and responsibilities for damage. For streets, delaying the final surface course until heavy construction is substantially complete helps produce a clean finished surface. Damage to the underlying pavement still needs to be evaluated and repaired as necessary. Transformers, pull boxes, hydrants, and similar facilities need visible protection. Marking fences help identify restricted areas, while locations exposed to vehicle strikes may need more substantial barriers.

## Significant infrastructure challenge (Q199)

One example involved relocating an approximately 60-inch concrete irrigation line that had served South Corona citrus groves for about 100 years. The existing line operated through elevation-driven hydraulic head in a siphon arrangement. Relocating it required careful attention to the system’s hydraulic behavior. The replacement design had to account for water levels and structures tall enough to accommodate the hydraulic head across the project. It was a specialized problem that required understanding how the existing irrigation system worked before designing its replacement.

## What builders should verify before treating a lot as ready (Q200)

Builders should verify approved plans, pad elevations, geotechnical acceptance, access, drainage, required utility availability, and inspection status. They also need to confirm that construction-stage stormwater protections are in place. These may include silt fencing, slope protection, mulch, and inlet protection. Use clear lot-readiness criteria so the builder knows what has been accepted, what restrictions remain, and whether any outstanding work affects the start of foundations.
