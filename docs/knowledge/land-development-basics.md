## What land development is

Land development turns a parcel of land into a property that can support homes, apartments, or another approved use. It includes studying what can be built, securing approvals, and preparing roads, drainage, utilities, and building areas.

Brian helps an owner make the best use of the land. That includes entitlements—the approvals needed to develop a property—and working with agencies on conditions that affect cost and responsibility. He works closely with engineers and other consultants to create a practical, functional, and cost-effective design.

Land development prepares the land and infrastructure. Building construction creates the homes, apartments, or other structures. The two must be coordinated from the beginning.

## What a land development specialist does

A land development specialist helps an owner understand the options, costs, risks, and steps needed to move a property forward. That includes making the best use of the land, improving the potential return, and controlling development costs through a realistic budget, a coordinated team, and a schedule that supports the owner’s goals.

## When to get involved

He can join before land is purchased, during planning and approvals, before construction, or after a project has problems. Earlier involvement gives more chance to identify risks before detailed plans and contracts.

The best time to call is usually before purchasing the land or committing to a specific design. At that stage he can help evaluate the proposed homes or apartments, infrastructure needs, development fees, and other major costs, including whether an investment project has a realistic path to net profit.

Common reasons builders and developers call: rising costs, missed deadlines, incomplete bids, unclear responsibilities, utility delays, plans that are hard to build, poor design, conflicts between plans, and agency conditions that add cost or responsibility.

## How the role differs

**General contractor:** Brian coordinates the owner’s broader goals, consultants, approvals, budget, and schedule. A general contractor manages construction under a defined contract. He can also help an owner arrange subcontracted or general-contracted site construction.

**Civil engineer:** The engineer prepares technical designs for grading, drainage, roads, and infrastructure. Brian coordinates those designs with approvals, costs, construction needs, and the owner’s goals. He has a degree in civil and structural design and more than 40 years reviewing engineering plans.

**Architect / landscape architect:** The architect designs buildings. The landscape architect designs landscaping and outdoor spaces. Brian coordinates that work with the land development plan, budget, and schedule, including MEP connections with civil and utility plans.

**Real estate broker:** The broker handles the property transaction. Brian evaluates development work, costs, risks, and infrastructure needs, including how conditions may affect a purchase.

**Owner:** The owner normally keeps funding, investment decisions, business goals, and major approvals. Payment authority and major financial decisions stay with the owner unless the agreement says otherwise. Brian can manage bidding, contracting, approvals, permits, construction, and completion for the owner.

## Connecting site work and buildings

He uses a coordinated plan and schedule covering lot readiness, foundations, utility connections, utility activation, and building completion.

Utility connections must be correctly sized and placed. He uses a combined utility plan, sometimes called a utility furniture exhibit, to coordinate sewer, water, storm drains, dry utilities, site lighting, and EV charging where included.

## Development management

Development management means overseeing land development from beginning to end: organizing the team, tracking approvals, reviewing budgets, managing schedules, coordinating decisions, and keeping the owner informed. He looks for ways to improve the design, cut unnecessary cost, and prevent gaps between responsibilities.

Hands-on work includes coordination meetings, deadlines, document review, follow-up, early value engineering, and plan-quality reviews.

He explains technical recommendations in plain language and ties options to cost, timing, and risk. Sketches or cross sections are used when helpful.

Immediate priorities: safety, required approvals, work that controls the completion date, major cost exposure, long-lead decisions, and plan quality. Each important issue needs a responsible person, a deadline, and follow-through.

## Cost and schedule drivers

Greatest effect on cost: building density, street layout, grading, retaining walls, utility extensions, and parking. Streets serving homes on both sides can use infrastructure more efficiently when the site allows it. On hillside or rocky sites, the amount and type of rock matters. Identify utility connection points early; long off-site extensions can be a major expense.

Greatest effect on schedule: approval strategy, environmental review, utility coordination, material lead times, contractor capability, and owner decisions. Entitlements and CEQA need active management. Potholing existing utilities before connecting can prevent redesign and delay. Good phasing can allow work to overlap.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with an incomplete budget. Even early on, every major cost category should be included, with allowances where amounts are not yet known.
- Assuming completed engineering plans are automatically practical or cost-effective.
- Missing early chances to improve grading, landscaping, utilities, and phasing.

Every piece of land is different. Similar home designs can repeat; grading, drainage, utilities, access, environmental conditions, and approvals do not.

## Named examples Brian supplied

**Chatsworth:** Two large bridges were needed to reach a development across a river. One required a steel superstructure because span and lifting constraints made concrete beams impractical. The work had to span protected habitat.

**Corona:** Two bridges were needed on a tight schedule. He selected large precast arch culverts with about 48-foot spans. The crossings were completed in about six months and at a lower cost than the conventional concrete bridge approach considered.

**Sewer lift station:** A subregional sewer lift station capable of serving about 3,800 units, including the 1,400-unit community being developed.

**Forestar-Chatsworth (Poema and Canoga) bridges** are also named in the John Quincy testimonial already on the site.

## First-time clients

First-time clients often do not know which questions to ask or which costs and risks may be missing. Brian helps them see the overall plan, expected costs, major decisions, and steps to completion. He explains details as the work moves forward.
