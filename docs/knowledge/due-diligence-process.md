# Due diligence process and early land-use checks

Source: Brian Wood Q&A, questions 81–108. Cleaned for site use. Do not invent fees, cities, or outcomes beyond this file.

## What land development due diligence is (Q81)

Due diligence is the investigation of a property’s rights, physical condition, approval requirements, and development costs before a major commitment. It helps an owner understand what is known, what still needs investigation, and what risks could affect the project. The findings also help a buyer determine what the land is worth based on its development potential and the cost of making it usable.

## Main steps (Q82)

Begin with the owner’s goals and the property records, then inspect the site, contact agencies and utility providers, and coordinate needed studies. Combine the findings into a development concept, cost range, schedule, and decision report. Unresolved issues receive clear follow-up assignments so the owner knows what must be addressed before moving forward.

## Property records reviewed first (Q83)

Review ownership and parcel information, the preliminary title report, recorded documents, maps, available surveys, zoning, and existing approvals. Check that the records cover the correct property and are current enough for the intended review. County and city property profiles can provide additional information. Contact utility providers to identify existing facilities near the project and determine what further investigation is needed.

## Boundary survey (Q84)

A boundary survey establishes the surveyed property lines and identifies related features within its scope, including possible encroachments from neighboring properties. It also provides a boundary framework that can be coordinated with survey control, topographic mapping, and preliminary design. Boundary conflicts or questions about ownership and easement rights may require additional review by title and legal professionals.

## Topographic survey (Q85)

A topographic survey is needed when elevations, slopes, drainage, roads, and existing improvements affect the development design. It provides the measured information used for grading, drainage, and site planning. Some flood control agencies and other public agencies have existing contour mapping that can support an early concept. The team must check its age, accuracy, and contour spacing. Detailed design requires survey information that meets the project’s needs and the reviewing agency’s standards.

## Easements (Q86)

Easements may reserve rights or space for access, utilities, drainage, irrigation, or other uses. Their recorded terms and locations must be reviewed before buildings, roads, or other improvements are placed. Older agricultural properties may have irrigation, flume, pipeline, or access easements crossing several parcels. These rights can be difficult to relocate or remove because multiple parties may benefit from them. Finding these easements early helps prevent major design changes later.

## Recorded access rights (Q87)

Recorded access rights must support the proposed development, including the location, type, and amount of use. A right to cross another property does not automatically include the right to install sewer, water, storm drains, or other utilities. Check whether the project has suitable public access or adequate private easements. The review must also address construction, operation, maintenance, and emergency access. Physical access and legal access both need to be confirmed.

## When title professionals should review questions (Q88)

Unclear ownership, conflicting property descriptions, missing easement documents, liens, access exceptions, and encroachments should be referred to title professionals and legal counsel as appropriate. Important questions should receive written clarification. The review must extend beyond the property being purchased. Off-site access routes, utility connections, and easements may cross other properties, and those rights need their own investigation.

## Existing improvements (Q89)

Buildings, pavement, drains, wells, tanks, and utility structures can reveal earlier uses and show where infrastructure may be located. They can also identify possible demolition needs, abandoned facilities, or conditions requiring further investigation. Historical aerial images, including available Google Earth imagery, can help locate former agricultural operations, structures, and possible storage areas. Image coverage varies by location and year. Suspected wells, fuel tanks, or pesticide storage areas must be checked through records, site investigation, and specialist review.

## Historical information and environmental risk (Q90)

Historical aerials, maps, permits, and use records can reveal former buildings, fill placement, farming operations, industrial activity, or changes in drainage. These findings help an environmental professional evaluate possible contamination concerns. A Phase I environmental site assessment may identify the need for a Phase II investigation. Phase II generally involves sampling and testing to investigate contamination. Its findings help determine whether further investigation or a cleanup plan is needed; it is not itself simply a mitigation plan.

## Geotechnical investigation (Q91)

Treat geotechnical review as an essential part of evaluating a development site. The review should consider previous reports, planned grading, foundations, slopes, existing fill, groundwater, rock, and earthquake-related hazards. A qualified geotechnical professional determines the field investigation needed. This includes evaluating concerns such as liquefaction and nearby faults. Regional maps provide useful background, but they do not replace a site-specific investigation when one is needed.

## Soil conditions (Q92)

Weak, expansive, collapsible, or variable soils can require removal, treatment, additional compaction, or special foundations. Rock and unsuitable fill can also increase grading costs. The geotechnical investigation should explain how these conditions affect design and construction. Those recommendations must be included in the development budget and schedule so the owner understands their effect on feasibility.

## Groundwater (Q93)

High groundwater can complicate excavation, trench stability, foundations, waterproofing, and underground utility installation. Sewer, water, and storm drain construction may require temporary dewatering or other measures that increase costs. Groundwater can also contribute to liquefaction risk in susceptible soils during an earthquake. The project team must evaluate these conditions and determine appropriate measures, including any requirements for treating or discharging pumped water.

## Drainage problems (Q94)

Review topography, existing drainage paths, flood information, upstream runoff, and the proposed discharge location with the civil engineering team. Site visits and aerial images can reveal drainage patterns, ponding, and erosion. Also check available regional drainage and flood control master plans. A project may need to accommodate or contribute to a planned drainage facility, which can affect both the layout and development cost.

## Whether utility service is available (Q95)

Contact each utility provider with the location and proposed development concept. Request information about existing facilities, possible connection points, service requirements, fees, ownership, and timing. Available sewer and water master plans can help identify existing and planned infrastructure. A nearby line or a line shown on a map does not confirm that the project can connect. The provider must verify the applicable service requirements.

## Whether available utilities have enough capacity (Q96)

Give providers the project’s expected demand and proposed construction phases, then request a capacity review and identification of needed upgrades. Ask what the response covers and whether additional studies or commitments are required. In Brian’s experience, dry utility planning reviews can take 30 to 60 days or longer, depending on the provider and project. Existing system maps help with early screening, but they do not replace the provider’s review. Sewer and water evaluations must also identify whether larger lines or other system improvements will be required.

## Environmental questions that need specialists (Q97)

Separate contamination concerns from habitat and natural resource concerns because they require different investigations. Former industrial activity, fuel tanks, dumping, stained soils, and pesticide storage may require an environmental site assessment. Habitat, waterways, protected species, and cultural resources require specialists in those areas. The planning department can help identify known constraints and applicable conservation plans. Depending on the site, coordination may also involve wildlife agencies, the Army Corps of Engineers, or other responsible agencies.

## Organizing findings for a decision (Q98)

Organize findings in an issue log showing the evidence, likely impact, recommended action, responsible person, and deadline. Confirmed constraints are separated from questions that still need investigation. Rank issues by importance. Matters that could stop the project, cause major delays, or add substantial costs come first. Design refinements and smaller cost questions follow. This helps the owner focus on the issues that matter before the next financial commitment.

## Preliminary feasibility review vs completed technical investigation (Q99)

A preliminary review uses available information to identify likely opportunities and constraints. A technical investigation includes defined fieldwork, analysis, and professional findings within an agreed scope. For example, a geotechnical investigation is more useful when the team has a preliminary grading and development concept. Proposed cuts, fills, buildings, and deeper basins help the geotechnical professional target the investigation. Without that information, the findings may be more general and require additional work later.

## Issues buyers overlook most often (Q100)

Buyers often overlook the difference between utilities being nearby and utilities being available with enough capacity. Connection locations, line sizes, off-site improvements, and the rights needed to reach those connections can greatly affect costs. Clear legal access and workable physical access are also critical. Subdivisions may require two separate access routes, depending on the project and applicable requirements. Access expectations should be confirmed early with the reviewing agencies and fire authority.

## Zoning (Q101)

Zoning helps determine allowed uses, density, building height, setbacks, lot coverage, and other development standards. It must be reviewed with the general plan, overlays, existing approvals, and applicable housing provisions. Also review agency design guidelines and engineering standards. These may address building layout, parking, landscaping, walls, streets, and utilities. A zoning label alone does not provide enough information to design a project.

## Allowed uses before detailed plans (Q102)

An owner should confirm whether the proposed use is allowed, what approvals it requires, and what conditions may apply. Zoning regulations and the general plan provide the starting point, while the planning department can clarify density ranges and review requirements. This review should happen before the owner pays for detailed plans. Early confirmation helps avoid designing a project around assumptions the agency will not support.

## Setbacks (Q103)

Setbacks determine required separation between buildings, property lines, streets, and other features. They can significantly reduce the buildable area, especially on narrow, irregular, or hillside parcels. Requirements vary by zoning, lot size, and development type. Side-yard setbacks and required distances between buildings must be checked separately. Also review whether the agency bases its requirements on gross lot area, net lot area, or usable land area.

## Height limits (Q104)

Height limits can affect the number of stories, roof design, building shape, and potential number of units. The team must confirm how the agency measures height, particularly on sloped sites. The review should also determine whether any project-specific approval or applicable housing provision changes the normal standard. Height limits need to be considered early in the building and grading concepts.

## Density limits on an apartment concept (Q105)

Density rules help establish the potential number of units, but the actual site layout may support fewer. Buildings must fit alongside parking, access, utilities, drainage facilities, landscaping, and required open space. Coordinate the land plan with civil engineers and the building design team to test a workable layout. High-density projects may need underground stormwater facilities or other space-saving designs. These choices must be evaluated for cost and maintenance before assuming the maximum density is practical.

## Parking (Q106)

Parking affects building placement, circulation, available open space, and the amount of land needed for development. It may also influence whether garages or structured parking are practical. Check the requirements for the specific project and location, including applicable housing provisions. Local zoning and design guidelines help establish dimensions, access, landscaping, and other layout standards. A single parking assumption should not be applied to every project.

## Lot coverage (Q107)

Lot coverage requirements limit how much of a site may be covered by buildings or other defined improvements. The local rules determine what counts toward coverage and which land area is used in the calculation. Distinguish between gross lot area, net lot area, and net usable area where those definitions apply. The design must satisfy the required coverage calculation along with setbacks, parking, landscaping, and other site requirements.

## Which planning department to contact (Q108)

Use the parcel number and official jurisdiction maps to confirm whether the property is within a city or in an unincorporated county area. A mailing address alone may not identify the correct planning authority. Also check service district boundaries and any relevant sphere of influence. A sphere of influence identifies a potential future boundary or service area; it does not by itself transfer current land use authority. It may still be relevant to annexation and service planning.
