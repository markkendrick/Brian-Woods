# Acquisition, feasibility, and site review

Source: Brian Wood Q&A (questions 12, 41–62, and 66–80). Cleaned for site use. Do not invent fees, cities, or outcomes beyond this file.

Use this file for `/services/land-development-feasibility-study/` and for acquisition copy on other URLs.

## Horizontal development and vertical construction (Q12)

Horizontal development prepares the land and infrastructure. It includes grading, roads, drainage, underground utilities, street improvements, and utility connections that serve the buildings.

Vertical construction includes foundations and the buildings themselves. As each home or apartment building is constructed, its systems connect to the site utilities.

The schedule must identify which improvements need to be ready before building construction begins. The budget should clearly define where each category starts and ends so work is neither missed nor counted twice.

## Before purchasing land (Q41)

Evaluate permitted use, legal access, title restrictions, boundaries, utilities, drainage, soils, environmental concerns, expected approvals, and total development costs.

The proposed product also needs to fit the local market. For apartments, look at achievable rents and the level of finish renters expect. For homes, look at likely selling prices and the quality buyers expect at those prices.

These factors help determine whether the development costs and potential income support the purchase.

## Information needed to start a site review (Q42)

Start with the address, parcel number, acreage, ownership or purchase status, intended use, target schedule, and available budget.

Also request surveys, title documents, plans, utility information, agency correspondence, and previous studies. Sellers may have useful information, but the buyer needs to ask for it.

If a tentative map has been prepared, there may be soils reports, drainage studies, and sewer and water studies. Raw land may have much less information. Reviewing existing documents helps identify gaps and avoid repeating work.

## Suitability for residential development (Q43)

Review land-use rules, usable acreage, road and emergency access, utilities, drainage, and ground conditions. Then work with the design team to test a layout and prepare an initial cost range.

Residential zoning alone does not mean the proposed project will work. Determine whether the intended development fits the zoning and general plan, or whether changes would be needed. Those changes can add substantial time and uncertainty.

Identify the nearest utility connection points. Long off-site extensions can significantly affect feasibility.

## Suitability for apartment development (Q44)

Review the allowed use and density, then test building placement, parking, circulation, open space, utilities, and fire access.

Apartment sites are often tight, so engineering and layout need careful coordination. Parking and EV charging requirements must be confirmed for the specific project because they affect space, electrical capacity, and cost.

Stormwater storage and treatment can take up substantial space and may require underground facilities.

Compare achievable rents with total development and operating costs. Permission to build apartments does not, by itself, make the project financially workable.

## What makes a site more attractive (Q45)

An attractive site has a suitable location, practical access, available utility capacity, manageable constraints, and development costs that fit the intended homes or apartments.

Topography makes a major difference. Flatter land is generally easier to develop. Greater elevation changes can require more grading and retaining walls while reducing the space available for buildings.

Location within a city also affects potential rents and home prices. Compare usable development potential, costs, and risks rather than looking only at acreage or purchase price.

## Inexpensive land that is costly to develop (Q46)

Low-priced land may require long utility extensions, major earthwork, retaining walls, access agreements, drainage improvements, or environmental cleanup.

In some cases, the cost of preparing the land is too high to support a practical purchase price.

Brian evaluates land through residual value. Starting with the expected value of the finished lots, he accounts for development costs, fees, financing, and the required return to determine what the project can support paying for the land.

A low asking price does not make a property a good deal if the improvements consume its development value.

## Properties without utilities (Q47)

Identify the service providers and investigate connection points, available capacity, required improvements, fees, and estimated timing.

For sewer and water, an early meeting with the district can help establish where facilities are and whether lines would need to be extended or increased in size.

Follow a similar process for electrical, gas, and communications. Evaluate the routes and property rights required to bring those services to the site.

A nearby utility line does not guarantee that the property can connect to it or that sufficient capacity is available.

## Limited road access (Q48)

Work with title and survey professionals to establish legal access. Then the design team evaluates whether the route can support construction traffic, everyday use, and emergency access.

An existing road may not provide the necessary rights, width, grades, or improvements for the proposed development.

Topography can add complications. Road construction may require cut-and-fill slopes outside the road right-of-way. Those areas may need permanent grading or slope easements from neighboring owners, which can add cost and uncertainty.

## Steep slopes (Q49)

Start with topographic information and geotechnical input. Then evaluate road grades, building pads, drainage, earthwork, and retaining walls.

Hillside design requires specific experience. Look for engineers who understand how to fit streets and lots to the terrain while controlling grading costs.

Understand the ground materials, including rock, boulders, and expansive soils. Deep cuts through rock can be especially expensive.

Comparing layouts early helps determine how much land is usable and which approach offers the best balance of cost and development potential.

## Oddly shaped parcels (Q50)

Begin with a measured plan showing the boundaries, setbacks, easements, access, and physical constraints. Then test where buildings, streets, utilities, and drainage facilities can fit.

Land planning quality is especially important on an irregular parcel. Civil engineering input should come early so the layout accounts for grading, drainage, and infrastructure from the beginning.

A skilled land planner can use the parcel’s angles and dimensions to create a practical arrangement of lots and streets. Total acreage alone does not show how much of the property can be developed efficiently.

## Previous use (Q51)

Ask about industrial activities, agriculture, dumping, fuel tanks, imported fill, wells, septic systems, and demolished buildings.

Historical aerial images can help reveal earlier uses, former buildings, or disturbed areas that deserve investigation.

Agricultural land may need evaluation for pesticide residues. Former dairy operations may require investigation of buried organic material and potential gas concerns.

An environmental professional should determine the appropriate assessment and any further testing. Findings can affect cleanup costs, timing, and the purchase decision.

## Neighboring properties (Q52)

Neighbors can affect access, drainage, utility routes, noise, privacy, and construction staging. Some improvements may depend on agreements with adjacent owners.

Understand how the city involves neighbors in its approval process. Public hearings, neighborhood consultation, and earlier agreements can influence the design and schedule.

Investigate these issues early so the owner understands which parts of the proposal may be affected and how those concerns could change scope or cost.

## Development capacity (Q53–Q55)

First review allowed density and land-use rules. Then work with the planning and engineering team to determine what can actually fit, accounting for streets, slopes, drainage, easements, utilities, parks, open space, and other constraints.

A good land planner can improve site use through careful street and lot layouts. The initial result should be a realistic range with clear assumptions. The final approved count depends on detailed design and approvals.

**Homes:** Review density limits, lot-size rules, parcel dimensions, setbacks, street standards, grading, utilities, drainage, and emergency access. Watercourses, riparian habitat, and other protected areas may reduce land available for development. The concept plan should show actual lots and the infrastructure needed to serve them. Dividing total acreage by the minimum lot size can give a misleading result.

**Apartments:** Review allowed density, unit mix, building height, layout, usable site area, parking, circulation, open space, and utilities. City design standards and applicable state and local requirements affect the specific proposal. The goal is how many units can fit in a functional design that meets requirements and supports the project financially.

## What not to assume from a listing (Q56)

Never assume that zoning allows the advertised use, nearby utilities have capacity, access is legally adequate, or an advertised unit count has been approved.

Check through due diligence: agency records, the city, utility providers, and a site visit. A visit may reveal physical constraints or existing utilities that need to be relocated.

## Comparing multiple sites (Q57)

Evaluate each property using consistent assumptions and cost categories: purchase price, potential lot or unit count, infrastructure costs, approval risks, schedule, and unresolved questions.

Every site has different constraints, so the lowest purchase price may not produce the best result. Look for the strongest balance of development yield, improvement costs, market potential, and manageable risk.

## More investigation vs walking away (Q58–Q60)

Further investigation may be needed for uncertain access, conflicting boundaries, possible contamination, undocumented fill, steep terrain, limited utility capacity, unresolved approvals, habitat impacts, or contaminated soils. Soil cleanup may require a mitigation plan, removal, and careful handling. Undocumented fill can increase grading costs. Steep terrain may require difficult road grades and substantial retaining walls. Rank concerns by potential effect and the time needed to obtain reliable answers.

Walking away may be appropriate when essential access cannot be secured, the intended use lacks a practical approval path, development costs exceed what the project can support, or sewer, water, or electrical service requires major system expansion that makes the project unworkable. The recommendation should come from documented findings and realistic alternatives.

Examples that changed a purchase decision include inadequate utility capacity, unusually high connection fees, or major environmental cleanup needs. On some properties Brian has evaluated, sewer and water fees alone could reach approximately $25,000 to $30,000 per unit. That is an example, not a standard fee. Significant contamination can also change whether a buyer proceeds. Identify cleanup scope, costs, responsibility, and completion requirements before the buyer commits. Where appropriate, recommend that the seller complete necessary cleanup before closing.

## Acquisition support (Q61–Q62)

Acquisition support includes site screening, consultant coordination, preliminary cost analysis, schedule review, and a written assessment of development risks.

The report identifies major concerns, possible solutions, and areas that need further investigation. It also explains where consultants should focus and whether specialist expertise is needed. The goal is a clear picture of what developing the property may require before a major commitment.

Most work begins with owners who already own land or have a specific property to evaluate. In some cases Brian also helps identify suitable development opportunities, for example apartment builders looking to expand their pipeline. The focus is development potential, constraints, and financial practicality.

## Initial feasibility review (Q66)

An initial review needs parcel records, the intended housing product, planning rules, available site studies, utility information, a preliminary lot or unit count, cost assumptions, and a proposed schedule.

The review should clearly distinguish verified information from estimates. It should also identify missing information and explain which findings could materially change the result.

## Whether a concept is realistic (Q67)

Test the concept against land-use requirements, physical constraints, infrastructure needs, construction methods, available funding, and market conditions.

Where there are uncertainties, bring in the appropriate specialist. Adjust the concept before treating it as a workable development plan.

## Assumptions that change the financial outlook (Q68)

Identify assumptions that could significantly affect the outcome, including unit count, grading quantities, utility capacity, approval timing, financing costs, and expected revenue.

Test reasonable changes to those assumptions, individually and together, to see which have the greatest effect on feasibility.

A realistic schedule is especially important. The time needed for entitlements, permits, and construction directly affects interest costs and how long the owner’s money remains invested.

## When detailed plans are not yet available (Q69)

Use parcel records, site visits, available mapping, utility-provider inquiries, and a preliminary layout.

Based on experience, Brian can prepare preliminary exhibits showing streets, sewer, water, and storm-drain layouts. These help establish likely infrastructure needs and support an initial cost estimate.

Then prepare cost and schedule ranges and identify the investigations needed to refine them. Conclusions should clearly reflect what remains unconfirmed.

## Purchase price vs development requirements (Q70–Q72)

Compare the purchase price with the full cost of approvals, site improvements, building construction, financing, and carrying the project through completion. That analysis helps determine what the finished project can support paying for the land while meeting the owner’s required return.

As improvement costs increase, residual value available for the land generally decreases. Sellers may have a price in mind that does not reflect those costs. A detailed development analysis gives the buyer a practical basis for discussing the purchase price.

Costs beyond the land purchase: consultants, entitlements, permits, agency fees, utility charges, off-site improvements, grading, environmental work, construction, financing, taxes, insurance, and contingency. For homes or apartments, include building costs using appropriate construction estimates, including cost per square foot where useful, plus selling expenses or apartment lease-up costs. The goal is the complete project cost, with a clear definition of what the estimate includes.

Off-site roads, drainage, and utility improvements can add substantial costs. They may also depend on agency approvals, property rights, or agreements with other owners. Evaluate likely scope, who is responsible, required rights, and time to complete. Agency conditions of approval can create off-site obligations. Allow contingency for unanswered questions. Those costs affect both feasibility and what the buyer can afford to pay for the land.

## Approval path and agency differences (Q73)

Approval requirements affect what can be built, which studies are needed, how long the process may take, and how much money must be committed before construction.

Work with the agency and consultants to establish the likely approval path. That helps the buyer align purchase decisions and funding milestones with the development process.

Agencies differ in procedures, review practices, and responsiveness. Those differences need to be reflected in the strategy and schedule.

## Letter of intent and purchase milestones (Q74–Q75)

During the letter of intent stage, identify development assumptions, information needs, and investigation time the buyer should consider. Prepare a detailed list of issues to investigate or resolve. That list helps define due-diligence scope, consultants needed, and a realistic review period. The buyer and their advisors can use it when establishing proposed transaction terms.

For purchase-agreement milestones, the buyer needs to understand which studies, agency responses, and utility confirmations can realistically be completed before each deadline. Prepare a detailed investigation and development schedule covering relevant approval, permitting, and construction steps. That helps identify unresolved risks before important decisions are due. The schedule also supports a cash-flow forecast showing when investigation and development expenses are expected. The buyer and their attorney can use this to evaluate purchase deadlines.

## Organizing due diligence (Q76–Q78)

Create a due-diligence checklist that identifies each issue, the responsible consultant, the required report or response, the cost, and the deadline. Prepare an initial due-diligence budget so the buyer understands expected expense and timing.

High-impact investigations start early. Track activities that depend on one another and hold review meetings before the buyer must make a contractual decision. More complex properties generally require more extensive investigations, which increases both budget and time.

When time is limited, investigate potential deal breakers first: legal access, intended use, major site hazards, utility capacity, and unusually expensive improvements. Studies with long lead times need to start promptly. Identify which questions can realistically be answered in the review period. Review each consultant’s scope and separate essential acquisition work from tasks that can wait until entitlements or later design. That helps control due-diligence costs while keeping attention on the purchase decision.

A preliminary site evaluation should clearly identify unconfirmed unit counts, soil conditions, access rights, utility capacity, fees, off-site obligations, approval timing, and development costs. For each unknown, explain its potential effect and what information is needed to resolve it. Zoning and a preliminary layout can support an initial unit estimate. Local soils engineers may provide useful background, but site-specific conditions still need investigation. Title documents help evaluate access. Utility providers can address capacity. Agency fee schedules are a starting point for fees. Identify each finding as preliminary or confirmed.

## What the client receives (Q79)

The client receives a site summary, preliminary concept findings, cost and schedule ranges, key risks, unresolved questions, and recommended next steps.

The review also includes the consultant reports and preliminary design information needed to address the main acquisition concerns.

The purpose is a clear basis for deciding whether to proceed, investigate further, reconsider the purchase terms, or stop.

## Promising opportunity vs paper-only (Q80)

A promising opportunity has a practical approval path, workable infrastructure, realistic costs, and homes or apartments that the market can support.

Its major assumptions have been tested through technical review, agency discussions, and financial analysis.

Look carefully at assumed increases in future sales prices or rents. If a project depends on rising values to become profitable, that needs to be clearly understood. Testing the project without assumed price growth helps show whether the opportunity remains workable if the market does not improve.
